# Reframer C — Tilt the paper

The exam paper is a spiral staircase seen from directly above. A complex number is one walker on that stair. Every problem in Test 1 and Test 2 asks the same three things: which paper to read, whether to send the walker around again, and which fate that walk has.

Throw the story map out. The SVG of stops, the thread lines, and the “same story” chips are a tour of rooms. Under a clock the student does not tour. They stare at one sentence and have to know, in a few seconds, what kind of sentence it is.

## Questions

These are the questions the current map does not ask. The pitch below is the answer I am willing to build.

1. When someone freezes on this course, is the missing piece a fact they never saw, or the recognition of which fact this sentence is asking for? The site is built as a list of facts with addresses. The exam is a stack of sentences. A night-before plan that walks fifteen rooms trains the list. It does not train the ten-second recognition.

2. What is a complex number in Howell & Mathews, once Chapters 1 through 4 are held at once? The opening move of the site is “a place and a move,” two addresses for one dot. That is true of Chapter 1 and it is already splitting the object in half. The dot on the paper is a footprint. The angle is which floor of a stair the walker climbed to leave that footprint. The modulus is how far the footprint sits from the pole. Cartesian form and polar form are two ways of writing the footprint down. Arg is the instruction “use the home floor, (−π, π].” arg is the whole stair. The point is neither of those names.

3. What is a function here? One rule for moving the walker. What is a sequence? The same rule, and the word again. What is a series? A second walker, the partial sums, fed by a stream of bags, the terms. Chapters 2 and 4 file these as different subjects. The mistakes are cross-wirings: the sequence test applied to the bags, the bag test applied to the walker, one application of f read as an orbit, an orbit read as a mapping.

4. What may colour mean? On the live site a single blue stands for a Cartesian address, a stayed pixel, a Newton root, and thread F2, and a single orange stands for a polar address, an escaped pixel, a Newton root, and the handle that is c. The wide review kept the picture colours and the thread colours apart with a sentence. The exam paper has no legend. The thing that has to be recognised is a word. If a picture needs paint, the paint can mean that word and nothing else.

5. Which confusions actually eat the first ten seconds?
   - arg, Arg, and the point.
   - A sum (square grid) and a product (the stair).
   - f(z) once, and f applied again.
   - The walker z and the slide c inside the rule. Julia’s plane and the plane of c are this confusion drawn at full size.
   - Bounded and convergent. Staying in some disk, and eventually sitting in every disk around a limit (Definition 4.1.1, Theorem 4.1.8).
   - “The terms go to 0” and “the series converges” (Theorem 4.1.17 is one direction).
   - The value f(z) and the fate of the orbit of z.
   - Which root the walk finds, and whether the walk leaves.
   - A derivative, which is one tiny move of an arrow stuck to the walker, and a Newton step, which spends that tiny move as a correction and then repeats it.
   - An open set, a coin that fits, and a limit, every path into the coin agreeing.

6. Is the set of c a new object? The book collects every c for which the orbit of 0 stays bounded, and says when a fixed point with |f′(z₀)| < 1 pulls neighbours in. The site’s seeds stop has been saying “there is no picture” loudly enough that the wide review had to demote that sentence. Refusing to draw the set teaches that the parameter is a different subject. It is the same walker, locked at the critical point, while the drag is handed to the rule.

7. Can Chapter 3 live in a picture whose thrill is iteration? Limits, the derivative, Cauchy–Riemann, and the harmonic section do not iterate. Forcing them into “fate” would fake the unity. They are the walker before anyone presses again: does the direction of arrival matter? A limit exists when it does not. A derivative exists when a tiny arrow spins and stretches the same way in every direction, with no mirror. A branch is the same question asked of the floor name. The real-line wall in the Newton half-plane example is the same question asked of a whole line of walkers. Iteration is the question you earn after the direction has stopped mattering.

8. What single image would make a roots-of-unity problem, a Cauchy–Riemann problem, a geometric series, and a Julia pixel feel like the same errand? A map of thirty-five rooms cannot. A camera on one walker can, if the student can tilt the exam paper up and see the floors.

## The pitch

Retire both homes. Retire the thread lines T1, T2, T3, F1, F2, and F3 as navigation. Keep every mathematical scene the stations already compute, and hang each one on one screen as a bookmark: a saved camera, a saved rule, a saved lock.

The space is the helicoid the staircase station has been hinting at and then flattening. Distance from the central pole is r = |z|. Height is the unwrapped angle. Looking straight down, height collapses, footprints cover each other, and you see the exam paper: the ordinary plane, Re across and Im up. Looking from the side, each extra 2π is a higher turn. The walker who goes once around the pole climbs one floor while their footprint, seen from above, moves smoothly. That is the whole of arg versus Arg versus z, felt in the wrist.

The stair the student sees uses r, not log r. Powers in this course are “r to the n, angle times n,” and circles |z − a| = R are round on the flat paper. A log-radius chart makes squaring into a doubling map and makes the unit circle a vertical line, which is beautiful and which makes addition and those circles unreadable. Log r is an overlay that turns on for one bookmark, the disk at c = 0, and stays off everywhere else.

Three beats sit above the picture and never leave. They are the recognition drill.

- **Which paper.** Square grid, when the sentence adds, subtracts, or talks about |z − a|, a circle not centred at 0, u and v, or partial derivatives. Stair, when it multiplies, divides, powers, roots, arguments, branches, |z|, or the unit disk of z².
- **How many times.** Once, for a mapping, a limit, a derivative, a single Newton correction written out. Again, for a sequence, an orbit, a basin, a series.
- **Which fate.** Four words, used in the book’s senses. *Settles*: eventually inside every disk around a limit. *Stays*: inside some disk, and maybe never sits down. *Leaves*: walks out of every disk. *Which*: which floor, which root, which address. “Which” is a different question from “whether.”

A series shows two marks, because it is two errands. The bags are the terms. The walker is the partial sums. Theorem 4.1.17 says that if the walker settles, the bags shrink to the pole. The section does not claim the converse, and the screen must not either.

Colour, if it is painted on the plane, means fate and nothing else. Blue stayed. Orange left. A third paint is the third root, and only on a Newton rule. The sentence under the green walker splits “stayed” into settles and stays, so a point on the unit circle under z² is not called a limit. Handles are shapes, so they can stop borrowing those paints: a round dot is the walker, a square notch is the slide c, a pin is a root. Dragging a notch changes the rule. Dragging a pin changes the targets. Dragging the dot changes the start. The orange-handle collision goes away because the notch has no fate colour.

The feeling to aim at: the sheet on the desk lifts, the footprint gains a floor, and the student says that the Arg jump, the branch cut, the eighth roots of unity, and the edge of the unit disk were the same cliff seen from above.

## The screen and the one gesture

One full-width picture. The default camera is orthographic, straight down, so the first paint is the plane they will see on the paper. A KaTeX line under it gives the Cartesian address, the polar address, the floor (a specific k in θ + 2πk), and the fate word. The rule is a sentence, not a diagram key: “square, then slide by c,” “one Newton correction for z² + 1,” “add the next bag zⁿ.”

The gesture is a vertical drag on a rail at the right edge of the paper. Dragging up tilts the camera from the exam sheet toward a side view of the helicoid. Dragging down lays it flat again. The tilt rail is a separate control from the walker, with the same manners the Julia handle had to learn: a press on the rail does not move anything until the pointer actually moves, and a press on the paper moves the walker. One gesture. A free orbit camera is the wrong thrill; it is how the 4D graph becomes a toy. Reduced motion gets a single button, “Show the floors,” that jumps the tilt and does not animate. Arrow keys move the walker. The button is keyboard reachable.

Pressing Again leaves a footprint and applies the rule once. Eight footprints are enough to see a cycle; the sequence page already had to disable the button after eight, and this screen keeps that cap. A “paint every start” switch fills the flat paper with fate colour, using the iteration the Julia and Newton pictures already run. The paint is off while the camera is tilted. The helicoid carries one walker and that walker’s footprints, not a fractal texture. Painting every orbit onto a 3D surface would hide the exam question inside a graphics stunt.

Floor lock is a switch. Locked, the walker lives on the home floor and the readout uses Arg; crossing the negative real axis jumps. Unlocked, height runs free and the footprint from above is unchanged. That switch is the branch chapter.

A recognition strip can sit under the picture in a later pass: a problem sentence from the existing index, and three taps (paper, times, fate) before the reveal. The first build does not need it. The picture has to make those three taps feel obvious first.

```
Which paper:  Square | Stair          How many:  Once | Again
Fate:  settles · stays · leaves · which

+------------------------------------------------+
|                                                |
|     flat exam plane, or the tilted stair       |
|     round walker, footprints, optional paint   |
|                                          |rail||
+------------------------------------------------+
| 3/4 + i/2     r = √13/4    floor 0 of arg      |
| rule: square, then slide by the notch          |
| drag:  walker  ·  notch c  ·  root pin         |
```

Bookmarks do not navigate away. `#stair` opens this screen. A query selects the scene: tilt, rule, lock, who is draggable, whether paint is on. The old hashes `#addresses`, `#julia`, `#sequence` can keep serving the old pages until someone cuts them over. The radical claim is about what the student meets first, and about what they practice the night before.

## Where every stop lands

Both homes become this screen. The Test 1 spine (place and move, staircase, motion, zoom) and the Test 2 spine (fate, square-then-slide, which root, the set of c) become the three beats plus the tilt. The night-before plan becomes: learn the tilt, then run the beats on the core sentences. Likely and possible bookmarks stay off that clock, as the wide review required of the sections themselves.

## What it would cost to build

One new static page in this repo. No backend, no new library. KaTeX is already how readouts are set. The plane, the complex arithmetic, the staircase drawing, the Julia walk, and the Newton step already exist in `js/core`. Three.js and OrbitControls are already vendored; the tilt should be a camera on a fixed arc, not a raw orbit control handed to the user.

First build, and no further:

1. A flat canvas for the exam paper, the walker, the footprints, the notch, and the pins. Fate paint reuses the existing iteration, on the flat camera only, and only while the user has asked for every start.
2. A helicoid mesh for the tilted camera, drawn with the one walker and the footprints. Radius is r. Height is unwrapped angle. The tilt rail interpolates between the two cameras. Reduced motion jumps.
3. The KaTeX line and the fate sentence. Floor lock. The rule sentence, fed by a small list of rules the stations already implement: slide, spin-and-stretch, mirror, reciprocal, zⁿ, z² + c, one Newton correction, partial sums of zⁿ.
4. Bookmarks as data: each existing stop id sets tilt, rule, lock, which handle is live, and whether paint is on. Old station modules stay where they are. This pass does not delete them and does not edit them.
5. The seeds paint: orbit of 0 across a coarse grid of c. The disk bookmark’s optional log-r overlay, one vertical line at r = 1 and the doubling explained in a sentence.
6. The flea inset reused from the derivative picture: a tiny arrow, or a tiny square, and its image. It opens for the derivative, Cauchy–Riemann, Newton step, attracting-fixed-point, and harmonic bookmarks. It stays closed otherwise.

Deliberately out of the first build: a fractal textured onto the helicoid, a second Mandelbrot illustration with its own palette, a recognition-quiz mode, any change to the old stations, any new colour for threads. Threads are gone on this screen, so they need no swatches.

The page should obey the constraints the reviews already paid for. A press does not jump a value before the drag. Eight steps, then the button stops. At a narrow width the page does not scroll sideways. Paint work stays off the interaction frames; the Julia film work already showed that a long task in a pointer handler is the failure mode. Hash routes that the old map owns keep working.

That is one station-sized module plus a preset table. The preset table is the real bulk, because every row has to be honest about paper, times, and fate. The geometry is the small part.

## The weakest point

The staircase makes products, powers, roots, arguments, branches, the unit disk, and a pure square feel like one object. Sums, distances that are not from the origin, the u-and-v calculus, and Cauchy–Riemann do not live on that stair. They live on the flat paper, in the flea inset. A student whose paper is mostly Chapter 3 tilts up, sees a beautiful helicoid, and still computes partial derivatives with no new help. The flea inset is the old derivative station, docked, not a consequence of the tilt.

So the unity is not “one surface.” The unity is one walker and three questions, and the stair is the right paper for only one of the two papers. If the page says the helicoid is the theory of Chapter 3, the pitch is false. The graph in four dimensions is the proof that it is false: we spent height on the angle’s name, and the graph of f needed that height for something else. That bookmark should look slightly apart, on purpose. Pretending otherwise is the way this reframing rots back into a tour of rooms with a prettier lobby.

The other way it fails is cheaper and more likely. “Stayed” and “settles” get painted the same blue, the notch that is c gets painted with a fate colour because the palette is sitting there, and the student is back to learning a legend. The words under the walker have to do the fine distinction. The shapes have to do the handles. The paint gets one job.
